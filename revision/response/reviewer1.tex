\section*{Reviewer 1}

\subsection*{R1.0 --- General assessment}
\refcom{Firstly, I find the paper challenging to follow, and its current structure
does not facilitate the reader's focus on the primary message. At this
point, I am unable to discern the central argument the authors intend to
convey.

The paper compares the IO model with two main configurations of CGEs, one
incorporating labour slack, both of which appear to embed capacity
constraints, characteristic not only of CGE models but also of other
macroeconomic models when short-run macro closures are applied. I find the
paper interesting and of interest to the reader of Metroeconomica. However I
have few points of improvements the authors would like to take into
consideration under revision stage.

I go straight to the main points that I believe need some restructuring.}

\response Before proceeding, let us emphasize that we agree with both reviewers that our
original narrative was somewhat ill-focused -- not only because, given parts
of our original framing, it did not spotlight the most important aspects, but
also because it led us to ignore certain foundational aspects that damaged the
credibility of the original text. Against this backdrop, we decided to rewrite
the whole draft, focusing on a more exhaustive and general treatment of our
main object of interest: the intersection between foundational assumptions of
input-output modeling and more pragmatic applications in the field of
transformational studies that rely on input-output models as a main workhorse.
While being more encompassing in terms of the model space discussed, this
fully rewritten new version allows for discussing our original question in a
more useful and better substantiated way.

Concretely, the revision restates the comparison as two primitive questions --- the
nature of the short run and the question of who bears the cost --- and organizes it
as a matrix of five labor-market closures and three financing closures, taking the
decarbonization of the German residential sector as an example for how input-output
modeling and transformation research might interact and how closure choices moderate
this interaction. The underlying framework is stated once for the open economy, with
imported intermediates and product taxes carried as explicit leaks rather than
absorbed into demand, and with the equivalence results that locate the two poles of
the closure family inside one model. Two extensions probe the limits of the
framework: a Kaldor--Verdoorn version in which technology responds to capacity utilization, and a
markup version in which profits enter as a second income stream. The appendix apparatus documents the model core, the equivalence proofs, the calibration and the data
pipeline.

In this light, we are extremely thankful for the constructive and direct
feedback received from the reviewers, which guided us in rethinking the main
setup of our narrative.
\loc{Sec.~1 (the thesis and the framing); Sec.~3 (the two primitive questions, the closure family and Table~2); Sec.~5 (aggregate and sectoral results).}

\subsection*{R1.1 --- Verbal description of the model; static vs.\ dynamic}
\refcom{First I would like to understand the structure of the CGE model. It would be
nice to say verbally what are the main features of the model. From my
understanding, the default model is a closed economy with full employment
and a hierarchical CES function. This setup results in a fixed labour supply
and fully flexible wages, essentially representing a conventional vertical
labour supply curve. Labour is the sole primary factor of production, so
capacity constraints originate here; the same would apply if capital stock
were included and held constant.

Another thing should be clarified is whether the model is static or dynamic.
Here my understanding is that the authors are running a static model with
fixed capacity. Basically a short-run economy with no adjustment towards a
new-steady state.}
\response{The revised manuscript now describes the model(s) extensively in words and conceptual terms before it is set up formally. Section~3 states the two closure questions and the family of labor-market closures they generate, and the opening of Section~4 states the static model core these closures share: a production network calibrated to an input--output table, cost-minimizing producers, a utility-maximizing household, and labor as the only primary factor (Sec.~4.2). Two things the comment asks about are now explicit. First, the model is static: the impulse is evaluated in a single short-run
equilibrium on a fixed production network. There is no transition and no
long-run equilibrium in the model -- no capital accumulation and no
intertemporal margin -- so the steady-state reading of the program is outside
its scope. Second, the characterization of the default model
as a closed economy in full employment with a vertical labor supply curve
describes one column of our matrix -- the standard CGE closure -- rather than
the paper as a whole, and the revision sets that closure beside four others,
including the elastic labor-supply and the employment-adjustment closures; the
calibration itself is (now) to the open economy of Germany, with imported
intermediates and indirect taxes booked as explicit leaks (see also our response to R2.5). To make room for the verbal exposition, some of the formal material (first-order conditions, proofs and the equivalence results) has been moved into two new appendices.}
\loc{Secs.~2--4; Apps.~A and B.}

\response{Thank you for requesting a clearer account of the calibration. Sections~4.1--4.2
explain how the model is calibrated to Germany's 2019 input--output table,
covering 71 sectors. Baseline prices are normalized to one, sectoral outputs
are expressed relative to aggregate value added, and observed expenditure and
cost shares determine the weights of the household and production CES
functions. The substitution elasticities are imposed separately; they are not
estimated from the input--output table. The step from the published table to the
calibrated shares --- the intermediate-use matrix, the import split, the cost shares and
the value-added decomposition --- is documented in Appendix~C, together with the
consistency checks it passes.

The calibration reproduces baseline sectoral gross output and domestic
household demand, with all goods markets clearing at the reference allocation.
This requires distinguishing domestic intermediate deliveries from imported
intermediates and product taxes: only the domestic intermediate bill generates
demand for domestic output. The household consumption shares and saving rate
are then calibrated consistently with this accounting. Appendix~C documents
these steps and the associated consistency checks.

One point of the accounting deserves an explicit correction. An earlier version
of this check netted the import and product-tax margins recorded on final use
rather than those recorded on intermediate use, which left an apparent
production--expenditure gap of about 5.4\,percent. Only the intermediate margins
belong in the deduction: the final-use margins are recorded in their own columns
and are not part of domestic final deliveries. With the intermediate margins
netted, expenditure-side GDP equals production and income exactly
(Appendix~C.7).

To make the calibration easier to assess, we have added two tables to the
appendices. The summary table in Appendix~C reports gross output, value added,
domestic intermediate use, imported intermediates and intermediate product
taxes, together with their shares and the three measures of GDP. The table in
Appendix~B.1 lists the elasticities used in the analysis, which are imposed
rather than estimated. We present these tables alongside the calibration
details to keep the main text focused on the comparison.}
\loc{Secs.~4.1--4.2; Appendix~C (the summary table) and B.1 (the elasticity table).}

\subsection*{R1.3 --- The Keynesian approach (Type I vs Type II multipliers)}
\refcom{The purpose behind the Keynesian approach in section 2.1 remains unclear to
me. It seems you are distinguishing between Type I and Type II multipliers
(see Miller and Blair, 2019). If I have interpreted this correctly, Type II
multipliers incorporate certain Keynesian features, which I agree, but the
ultimate objective of this distinction needs clarification.}
\response The revised manuscript does not use the Type I/Type II vocabulary as a framing
device -- the paper's first question is now stated as the choice of the short
run -- but the distinction does map onto the model, and it is worth saying
how. On the standard definition our model is a Type II model throughout: section 2 states the extension of the inter-industry table to household consumption and applies it throughout, and household consumption depends on disposable resources --- the wage bill net of taxes and the net external transfer --- so the matrix that generates our multipliers carries that household round. Appendices~A and~B state and derive the equivalence on exactly that closed system. On categorization we would add that while a Type II model is sometimes conceived as ``more Keynesian'' due to higher multipliers (for wage-pinned closures), the Type I closure would assume that all additional income resulting from additional spending leaks, which is a key reason why it had fallen somewhat out of fashion.
\loc{Sec.~2 (the household-consumption extension); Sec.~4 (the household block); Apps.~A and~B.}

\subsection*{R1.6 --- Aggregate vs.\ sectoral results}
\refcom{I would also try to differentiate and stress particularly more the
differences between the results of the aggregate economic variables and the
sectoral results. For the type of shock the authors are doing and given the
model configuration adopted, in aggregate the economy is not sensitive to
change in elasticity of substitution while these changes in price
sensitiveness it matter a lot for sectoral outcome,}
\response{Thank you for this suggestion. The revision separates aggregate results
in Section~5.1 (Table~3) from sectoral results in Section~5.2
(Figures~2--3), with detailed cell-by-cell panels in Appendix~D.
The distinction matters because a small aggregate response can conceal
substantial changes in the composition of production and in sectoral prices.
For example, under no reallocation, glass output rises by about 12--14\,percent while its price rises by 22--28\,percent; with elastic sectoral labor
supply, the corresponding increases are about 17--18\,percent in output
and 10\,percent in price. These are differences between labor-market
closures at the reported substitution elasticities, not estimates of
substitution-elasticity sensitivity.

We also qualify the role of substitution elasticities. In the common-wage
closures at baseline technology, the demand-only shock leaves relative
prices unchanged, so varying substitution elasticities cannot generate a
substitution response. With sectoral wages, prices do move and household
substitution becomes relevant, which is why the sectoral results in
Section~5.2 report the consumption response alongside output and prices.
The paper does not claim general aggregate insensitivity to every
substitution parameter.

Section~6 then shows the boundary of the demand-only equivalence: once
productivity or markups respond to demand and prices move, the
employment-adjustment and Leontief closures no longer generally coincide
(Tables~5--6). The revised presentation thus distinguishes aggregate from
sectoral outcomes and identifies the conditions under which substitution
can affect them.}
\loc{Secs.~5.1--5.2, Table~3 and Figures~2--3; Appendix~D (sectoral panels); Sec.~6, Tables~5--6 (pricing extensions).}

\subsection*{R1.7 --- Labor-market slack and the wage-setting rule}
\refcom{The discussion regarding labour market slack requires additional
explanation. The authors mention analysing various forms of labour market
slack, including a demand-driven CGE model and a calibrated approach. In the
demand-driven scenario, an exogenous increase in employment is introduced
based on input from the IO model. I am unsure about the rationale behind
this scenario. Even absent a model, it is apparent that GDP would rise
proportionally with employment increases, especially since price effects do
not translate into changes in competitiveness. While at the aggregate level,
differences between CGE and IO models are minimal and expected, at the
sectoral level, outcomes may vary significantly due to price sensitivity,
which depends on substitution elasticities.

Although I do not perceive any advantage in the demand drive scenario above
concerning labour market slack, the calibrated approach seems more
promising. However, several aspects remain unclear. This model evidently
allows for involuntary unemployment, resulting in an imperfect labour
market. Accordingly, a wage-setting rule must be specified, yet the authors
have not addressed this. Wages should no longer be determined solely by
profit maximization; instead, some wage-setting mechanism such as fixed real
or nominal wages should be implemented. Also a simple wage curve.

Unless I have misunderstood the authors' approach, is it simply a matter of
increasing labour supply by the number of unemployed persons, similar to the
previous scenario but with different figures?}
\response{The reviewer is right that the two original designs did not specify a
labor-market mechanism: they injected additional factor resources
exogenously, using either the IO result or an unemployment-based estimate.
We have dropped both designs. The revised comparison instead states the
labor-market closure explicitly and lets employment follow from it.

In the employment-adjustment closure, three choices must be distinguished.
The common money wage is normalized to $w=1$, which fixes the unit of
account. The aggregate labor-supply constraint is dropped, so employment is
endogenous, uncapped and determined by the labor required for equilibrium
production. Separately, the net external transfer is fixed at $F=0$.
Employment accommodation and the external-transfer restriction define the
closure; the wage normalization alone does not supply a behavioral
wage-setting mechanism. This is an assumption of available factor resources,
not an estimate of how many unemployed workers can be mobilized.

The role of the real wage also needs qualification. In the common-wage,
one-factor constant-returns price block, relative prices and the real wage
are determined by technology. At baseline technology, a demand-only shock
therefore leaves the real wage at its reference value; it is not held there
by an independently imposed real-wage peg. Appendix~B.5 states this result,
and Appendix~B.2 records that the wage pin is a numeraire rather than a wage-setting rule, so a rule determining only the nominal scale cannot generate an additional real adjustment margin within that price block. These statements do not extend to the sectoral-wage closures or to
the demand-dependent pricing extensions of Section~6.

The elastic labor-supply closure provides a different adjustment mechanism:
each sector has its own wage and a supply curve in the CPI-deflated real
wage, with an explicit supply elasticity. It is a reduced-form supply
relation, without a labor--leisure income effect, rather than an estimated
unemployment wage curve. We therefore distinguish this closure from the
employment-adjustment benchmark and do not claim to implement the wage-curve
mechanisms discussed in the applied literature.}
\loc{Sec.~3, Table~2 (closure comparison); Appendix~B.2 (the labor closures and the wage pin as numeraire) and B.5 (the demand-only reduction and the real-wage anchor); Sec.~6 (pricing extensions).}

\subsection*{R1.8 --- Labor mobility and the unified wage rule}
\refcom{Another question pertains to labour mobility. Under imperfect labour market
conditions, does the model assume a unified wage rule to enable labour
mobility across sectors?}
\response{In the original version, labor supply was specified as a sector-specific quantity with a cap in every sector ($l_i \leq l_i^{\max}$), and the wage was sector-specific as well -- in the standard scenario and in both slack variants; a unified wage rule never appeared. That specification is exactly what the revised matrix now reports as its no-reallocation column. The case the comment asks about -- labor moving
across sectors at one flexible wage with total employment fixed -- is new in
the revision, where it is the standard CGE closure and the reference against

reallocation parameter runs from the immobile case to full reallocation, and
full reallocation coincides with the conventional CGE closure, so that the
treatment of labor mobility is now an explicit choice rather than an inherited
default. Section~3 states the family; Appendix~B derives each closure and
their overlaps.}

\subsection*{R1.10 --- McGregor, Swales \& Yin (1996)}
\refcom{I also advise the author to read this paper:

Peter G. McGregor, J. Kim Swales, Ya Ping Yin. 1996. A LONG-RUN
INTERPRETATION OF REGIONAL INPUT-OUTPUT ANALYSIS. Journal of regional
science.

In this paper you will see that if you allow for involuntary unemployment
and full economic adjustment you will get into a new steady state with
IO-Type of results. That is with zero changes in prices in the long-run.
Naturally in the long-run multiplier will be much larger than an IO case.}
\response{We are grateful for this reference, which provides a useful dynamic
perspective on demand-determined quantities. The connection to our
employment-adjustment closure and its Leontief fixed-coefficient variant is
that output accommodates demand while prices remain unchanged under the
baseline demand-only shock. Our static benchmark equals the corresponding
household-closed input--output multiplier; it does not establish a multiplier
larger than that benchmark.

The distinction is the adjustment process. Our model compares static
equilibria at a single horizon, without investment-driven capacity growth or
a transition to a new steady state. We therefore cite
\citet{McGregor.1996} in Sections~3 and~7 as a perspective for a dynamic
reinterpretation of the comparison, rather than as a mechanism or long-run
result reproduced by our model.}
\loc{Secs.~3 and~7; Appendix~B (the demand-only multiplier equivalence).}
