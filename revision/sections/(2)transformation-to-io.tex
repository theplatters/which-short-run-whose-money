\section{From transformation research to input--output models}\label{sec:io-bridge}

Proposals for decarbonization differ widely in ambition, from modest initiatives that step up existing programs to large-scale suggestions for a fundamental socio-ecological transformation. What the two ends of this range share is that both imply a repurposing of spending, by private agents, by the public sector, or by some combination of the two. In that respect such programs resemble traditional public spending programs (e.g.\ \citealp{Pollin.2015, Mazzucato.2013}), and they invite the same question: what are the economic consequences of such repurposed or additional spending?

The recent literature exploits this similarity in several rather different ways. One strand is technocratic in tone: public spending is presented as a functional means, or even a necessary requirement, for decarbonization and socio-ecological transformation \citep{Mazzucato.2013, Mazzucato.2018}. A second strand is political: on the same basis, public spending is argued to alleviate environmental degradation and social exclusion at once, and thus to serve as a keystone of a just transition \citep{NewellMulvaney.2013}. A third, more analytical strand uses input--output models to identify structural dependencies and critical junctures, and thereby to assess the resilience and versatility of economies in times of intense transitions \citep{Farmer.2024, BF2019, Acemoglu.2012}. 

An input--output model, in its simplest form, records the inter-industry transactions of an economy in a matrix $A$ of input coefficients and relates total output $y$ to final demand $f$ for all sectors through $y = Ay + f$, hence $y = (I-A)^{-1}f$ \citep{Leontief.1936, leontief1986input, BlairMiller}. The Leontief inverse $(I-A)^{-1}$ is an inter-industry multiplier: a shift in final demand propagates along the supply chains of the economy. Reading a policy impulse as a shift in $f$ is the traditional application of such a model. It can be read as a sectoral analogue of the Keynesian cross \citep{Keynes.1936}, substituting an inter-industry multiplier for the household multiplier, or complementing it.\footnote{Adding information on wages and consumption shares allows for extending the inter-industry table to also incorporate household consumption \citep[ch.~6]{BlairMiller}, which is applied throughout the paper.} The analogy also shows where a plain Leontief application falls short. The classical debates about public spending -- the balanced-budget result of \citet{Haavelmo.1945}, who notes that the state, unlike the household, spends all of its income, or the ``crowding out'' of private activity discussed by \citet{BlinderSolow.1973} -- turn on two questions that such an application answers by assumption: where the spending comes from, and which resources it releases or absorbs. Our comparison asks which perspective is adequate to a socio-ecological transformation, which is why we connect transformation research to several input--output models instead of a single one.

Our exemplary application goes to the heart of a key issue in transformation debates, namely the decarbonization of the residential sector in a large economy with a strong dependence on fossil energy. We start from a concrete estimate for additional investment requirements in Germany to arrive at a carbon-neutral residential sector by 2050 \citep{Hornykewycz.2025}. Such an effort would require more than doubling spending on renovation and increasing annual expenditures on this by about € 58 bn per year on average (in 2023 prices), focused on a small subset of all sectors.\footnote{The following sectors are subject to demand shock (with the expected relative change in final demand for that sector given in brackets): Glass and glassware (+50.3\%), ceramic products and processed stones (+37.3\%), specialized construction work (+27.8\%), rubber and plastic products (+13.4\%), chemicals and chemical products (+3.7\%), machinery (+1.2\%) and electrical equipment (+1.0\%).} The program falls predominantly on construction services and materials.\footnote{Their provision rests on occupations that for the most part already exist: only a small share of green-driven occupations are new or emerging; the bulk are existing occupations whose task content is changing or which are simply in greater demand \citep{Bassanini.2024, Tyros.2023, Bachmann.2026, Wehden.2026}.} In 2019 prices, the base year of the input--output table, this corresponds to € 40.3 bn, or 1.33\% of GDP, where GDP throughout is the modelled sectors' gross value added exclusive of taxes on final use. We write this program as a vector $g$ of additional real final demand by sector, $g_i \geq 0$, so that in the plain Leontief reading it simply adds to final demand, $y = (I-A)^{-1}(f+g)$, and costs $\sum_i p_i g_i$ at prices $p$. Who bears this cost is left open in that reading; the financing closures of Section~\ref{sec:two-questions} are three answers to exactly this question.

The program spending does not fall on domestic output alone. On the 2019 table, about $18\%$ of the program's bill is imported final use and about $9\%$ is product taxes, so about $73\%$ of it is demand for domestic production; the imported intermediates that this production then requires add a further $17\%$ of the program's size, bringing the total import content to roughly $35\%$, while the value added generated along the supply chains amounts to about $56\%$ of the program's size (Appendix~\ref{app:pipeline-composition}). Imports and product taxes are therefore carried as explicit leaks: only the domestic part of the bill counts as demand for domestic output (Section~\ref{sec:application} and Appendix~\ref{app:closure-systems}).
